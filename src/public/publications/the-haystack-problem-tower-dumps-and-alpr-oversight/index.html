The Haystack Problem

What a federal tower-dump ruling can—and cannot—tell Arkansas about ALPR oversight

Publication scope

This is a tower-dump case, not an ALPR ruling. The comparison identifies structural questions, technological differences, contrary ALPR authority, and limits on what the Sherwood records establish.

Published: August 17, 2026

On August 5, 2026, U.S. District Judge Carlton W. Reeves upheld the denial of federal applications for “tower-dump” warrants. Those warrants would have required cellular providers to identify devices connecting to towers near several crime scenes. Investigators hoped to compare the resulting records and identify people who appeared near more than one location.

The court acknowledged that the technique could produce valuable investigative leads. It also found that the government would obtain records belonging to countless people who happened to be near the selected locations at the “wrong” time. Judge Reeves concluded that tower dumps are unconstitutional general warrants and denied the government’s request.

This is not an automatic-license-plate-reader case. It does not declare Flock Safety, ALPR cameras, or every historical vehicle search unconstitutional. It is a federal district-court ruling from Mississippi and is not binding precedent in Arkansas.

But the ruling addresses a question that cannot be dismissed with the phrase “it’s just a license plate”:

When government collects or searches a large body of innocent people’s location records to identify an unknown suspect, does narrowing the results afterward cure the breadth of the original search?

For tower dumps, Judge Reeves answered no. Arkansas policymakers should examine why the same structural question matters when ALPR detections are stored, searched, sorted, shared, and cross-referenced across cameras and jurisdictions.

The investigation and the requested data

The litigation began with four federal search-warrant applications involving an investigation into violent crimes in the Jackson, Mississippi, area. According to Magistrate Judge Andrew Harris’s February 21, 2025 order:

  • The government sought records from four cellular providers.
  • The requested records concerned towers serving nine locations.
  • Individual time windows ranged from ten minutes to one hour.
  • The requests collectively covered 220 minutes across the locations and 880 provider-minutes when multiplied across the four companies.
  • The government acknowledged that the production would be voluminous and include identifiers belonging to uninvolved people.
  • Investigators intended to compare the records to identify or eliminate potential suspects.

Magistrate Judge Harris denied the applications. The government later returned with narrower applications involving six locations, shorter time windows, and devices appearing at two or more locations. Those applications were also denied.

Judge Reeves reviewed both approaches. His August 2026 order concluded that the narrower design did not solve the underlying Fourth Amendment problem: investigators still lacked individualized probable cause for the devices and people whose records would be searched.

The court described the problem with a simple metaphor: government cannot claim access to an “entire haystack” merely because the haystack might contain a needle.

Why the court called it a general warrant

The Fourth Amendment was written in direct response to general warrants—broad authorizations that allowed officials to search first and decide what was relevant afterward. Modern technology changes the scale and speed of that process, but not necessarily the underlying constitutional concern.

The court’s reasoning had three parts.

1. Location data can expose more than one isolated observation

Cellular tower records can indicate when a device was near homes, businesses, medical facilities, places of worship, political offices, highways, or other sensitive locations. Even when each individual record represents a limited period, cross-referencing locations can reveal relationships and patterns that no single observation would disclose.

2. Probable cause must precede the search

The government argued that the records could identify the unknown perpetrators. The court distinguished investigative usefulness from individualized probable cause. Evidence that someone involved in a crime probably used a phone does not establish probable cause to obtain location information for every device in the surrounding area.

The government cannot use a mass search to discover which people it should have had probable cause to search in the first place.

3. Narrowing the results afterward does not necessarily cure the initial sweep

The revised applications attempted to reduce the final pool by seeking devices detected at multiple locations. The court concluded that post-collection filtering did not make the initial access particularized. A search does not become narrow solely because investigators promise to focus later on a smaller subset.

The NACDL, ACLU, ACLU of Mississippi, Electronic Frontier Foundation, and Mississippi Office of the State Public Defender developed this argument in their joint amicus brief. They emphasized that reverse-identification techniques begin without a known target and use a broad population’s information to generate one.

Where the ALPR comparison fits

Tower dumps and ALPR systems collect different information. A cell phone can follow its user into homes and other private spaces. A stationary ALPR ordinarily records a vehicle only when it passes a camera, usually on a public roadway. That distinction is legally significant.

The technologies nevertheless share an investigative structure:

Tower-dump process Networked ALPR process
Obtain records for many devices near a place and time Store detections of vehicles passing fixed or mobile cameras
Most records concern people not suspected of a crime Most detections concern vehicles not connected to a crime
Begin without knowing every suspect’s identity Historical or vehicle-description searches may begin without a confirmed plate
Compare devices appearing at multiple locations Compare plates, vehicle attributes, cameras, dates, times, and jurisdictions
Use the dataset to generate suspects or witnesses Use the dataset to generate investigative leads
Later narrow a large production Filter stored detections after collection
Potentially reveal presence near sensitive places Potentially reveal routes, routines, associations, or probable destinations

The responsible comparison is therefore not “tower dumps and ALPRs are identical.” They are not.

The responsible comparison is this:

Both technologies can reverse the traditional sequence of investigation by collecting or accessing many people’s location records first and using the resulting dataset to determine who deserves further scrutiny.

That is the “haystack problem.”

What Sherwood’s own release demonstrates

The concern is not limited to a hypothetical nationwide system. Records released by the City of Sherwood demonstrated what can be learned from ordinary ALPR fields.

The city released a spreadsheet containing multiple detections associated with a single vehicle that was not registered to the requester. When sorted by date and time, the records showed:

  • repeated observations across multiple days;
  • camera locations;
  • timestamps;
  • direction or likely direction of travel; and
  • changing vehicle classifications associated with the same plate.

The production also included 85 unredacted vehicle images. Other Sherwood productions contained additional unredacted license-plate numbers.

One row may look like an isolated observation. Multiple rows can form a timeline. Camera locations and directions can suggest a route. Repeated observations can suggest recurring travel windows or habits. Searching surrounding cities or a broader shared network could expand that picture.

This does not prove that Sherwood officers improperly tracked the vehicle. It does not prove that every outside network search returned, displayed, or downloaded Sherwood data. It does prove that stored ALPR records can be organized into information about movement—and that the privacy implications cannot be evaluated by looking at one plate image in isolation.

Sherwood’s network exports also require careful description. One analyzed production contained 2,306,763 organization occurrences across 3,563 organizations. Those figures document scale, not confirmed access. An organization’s appearance in a network export does not, by itself, prove that the organization viewed Sherwood records, received a responsive detection, or engaged in misuse.

That distinction is central to evidence-based oversight: scale identifies the questions that audits must answer; it is not a substitute for the answers.

Arkansas law already treats ALPR data as regulated information

Arkansas’s Automatic License Plate Reader System Act does not treat captured plate data as consequence-free simply because a license plate is displayed publicly.

As amended by Act 668 of 2025, the statutory framework includes the following controls for governmental ALPR use:

  • Authorized use: Arkansas Code § 12-12-1803 identifies the permitted governmental uses of ALPR systems.
  • Purpose limitation and ordinary retention: Under § 12-12-1804(a), captured plate data generally cannot be used or shared for another purpose and cannot ordinarily be preserved by eligible governmental entities for more than 150 days.
  • Extended-retention deletion: Section 12-12-1804(b) permits investigative retention but requires destruction when an investigation ends without charges or when the related criminal action concludes.
  • Sharing threshold: Section 12-12-1804(d)(2) permits law-enforcement sharing when captured plate data indicates evidence of an offense.
  • Public reporting: Section 12-12-1805(a) requires covered users to compile reviewable statistical data every six months and preserve it for eighteen months.
  • Required statistics: Section 12-12-1805(b) calls for the number of plates scanned, lists checked, confirmed matches, non-correlating alerts, and matches resulting in arrest and prosecution.
  • Public rules: Section 12-12-1805 requires publicly inspectable rules addressing how data is obtained, retained, and destroyed, including data kept beyond 150 days.

These requirements apply independently of whether a particular ALPR use violates the federal Constitution. Federal constitutional law is a floor, not a replacement for Arkansas’s statutory safeguards.

The reporting requirements are especially relevant. A system cannot be evaluated solely by counting detections or alerts. The statute requires information that connects system activity to confirmed matches, false or non-correlating alerts, and enforcement outcomes. If those fields are not recorded contemporaneously, a later six-month report may not be reproducible or auditable.

What existing ALPR cases say

Federal courts have generally upheld the specific ALPR uses presented to them, especially when the database returned only scattered observations on public roads. That authority must be included in any honest assessment.

Decisions favoring limited ALPR access

  • United States v. Porter, 170 F.4th 381 (5th Cir. 2026): The Fifth Circuit held that a relatively sparse local network producing periodic public-road observations did not reveal the whole of the defendant’s movements. This is persuasive, not binding, in Arkansas.
  • United States v. Jiles, No. 8:23-cr-98 (D. Neb. Feb. 29, 2024): A query producing approximately five or six observations was held insufficiently comprehensive to constitute a search.
  • Schmidt v. City of Norfolk, 819 F. Supp. 3d 492 (E.D. Va. 2026): The court granted summary judgment to Norfolk after finding that detections from 176 cameras, retained for 21 days, still contained substantial gaps and did not reconstruct the whole of the plaintiffs’ movements. The decision is on appeal.
  • Scholl v. Illinois State Police, 776 F. Supp. 3d 701 (N.D. Ill. 2025): The district court rejected a Fourth Amendment challenge to a large expressway-camera program, concluding that the system did not create the comprehensive tracking at issue in Carpenter. The appeal remains pending.

Why those cases do not end the inquiry

Those decisions are fact-specific. They evaluate the density of the camera network, number and frequency of observations, retention period, geographical reach, search performed, and whether the resulting data reconstructed the whole of a person’s movements.

They do not establish that:

  • every ALPR network is constitutional;
  • 30, 90, or 150 days of retention is automatically constitutional;
  • nationwide or multi-jurisdictional retrospective searching is categorically exempt from the Fourth Amendment;
  • vehicle-description, convoy, pattern, or route searches are legally identical to checking a known plate;
  • an ALPR alert is self-verifying; or
  • compliance with the Fourth Amendment automatically satisfies Arkansas law or local policy.

The Supreme Court has not decided whether warrantless historical access to an ALPR database is a Fourth Amendment search. The Eighth Circuit, whose decisions bind federal courts in Arkansas, has not decided that question either.

The Supreme Court’s new location-data guidance

Judge Reeves’s order relied heavily on the Supreme Court’s June 29, 2026 decision in Chatrie v. United States. Chatrie involved a geofence warrant directed to Google, not an ALPR database. The Supreme Court held that police conducted a Fourth Amendment search when they acquired the defendant’s Google Location History.

The Court emphasized that:

  • private-company custody does not automatically eliminate Fourth Amendment protection;
  • retrospective access to an existing location database can constitute a search;
  • short duration does not automatically remove location data from constitutional protection; and
  • selecting a limited production from a much larger database does not alone answer the constitutional question.

But Chatrie also emphasized features of cellphone data that may distinguish it from ALPR records: extraordinary precision, frequent recording, entry into private spaces, and the close relationship between a phone and its user.

The decision therefore strengthens scrutiny of reverse-location searches without automatically deciding the ALPR question.

What the Mississippi ruling establishes—and what it does not

Supported by the ruling Not established by the ruling
Investigative usefulness does not itself establish constitutionality. That any Arkansas ALPR program is unconstitutional.
A short time window does not automatically cure a reverse-location search. That cell-tower records and plate detections receive identical legal treatment.
Searching many innocent people’s data to identify an unknown suspect raises general-warrant concerns. That Judge Reeves’s decision binds Arkansas courts or officials.
Post-collection filtering may not cure an overbroad initial search. That every historical ALPR search requires a warrant under existing Eighth Circuit law.
Cross-referencing locations can make isolated records more revealing. That appearing in a network audit proves an agency accessed Sherwood data.
Database design, scope, access, retention, and search methods matter. That one favorable outcome eliminates the need for reporting and oversight.

The accountability questions Arkansas should ask

The court filings and Arkansas records support specific questions rather than a predetermined conclusion:

  1. Known target: Did a historical search begin with a confirmed plate or identified suspect, or was it designed to generate candidates from vehicle characteristics, locations, or patterns?
  2. Documented basis: What case number, incident number, facts, and authorized purpose supported the search?
  3. Scope: How many cameras, jurisdictions, networks, days, and detections were searched?
  4. Search method: Was the query a known-plate search, vehicle-description search, convoy search, pattern search, route reconstruction, or another form of analysis?
  5. Results: How many records were returned, and how many belonged to vehicles with no demonstrated connection to the investigation?
  6. Access: Which users and agencies viewed, downloaded, exported, or received responsive records?
  7. Retention: When will ordinary and investigation-retained records be destroyed, and is deletion documented?
  8. Sharing: Did shared data meet Arkansas’s “indicates evidence of an offense” threshold?
  9. Verification: Were alerts visually confirmed and checked through ACIC/NCIC before enforcement action?
  10. Auditability: Can six-month statistics be reproduced from contemporaneous source records and reconciled to searches, alerts, dispositions, arrests, and prosecutions?

These questions do not prevent police from using legitimate investigative tools. They determine whether the tools operate within documented, reviewable boundaries.

Conclusion

The question is not whether a license plate can be seen from a public road. The question is what government can learn after millions of public observations are stored, searched, sorted, shared, and cross-referenced.

The Mississippi court called this the haystack problem. Sherwood’s own production demonstrates why the issue is relevant: once routine detections are organized by plate, date, time, camera, and direction, “just a license plate” can become a record of movement.

That does not make every ALPR use unconstitutional. It makes accurate reporting, narrow access, documented purposes, controlled sharing, definite deletion, independent verification, and meaningful audits essential.

Arkansas law already requires many of those safeguards. The immediate accountability question is not whether ALPR technology has ever helped solve a crime. It is whether agencies can demonstrate—through the records the law requires—that the system’s millions of ordinary detections are being used, shared, retained, and destroyed within enforceable limits.
